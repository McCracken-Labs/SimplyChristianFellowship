The Two Commands That Hold Everything

A lawyer tried to pin Jesus down to one commandment, and Jesus handed him two that refuse to be pulled apart.

This Week’s Devotional

❦

“Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets.”Matthew 22:39-40, KJV

When a religious teacher asked Jesus to name the greatest commandment, he answered with two. Love God with your whole heart, mind, and soul, and love your neighbor as yourself. He said the second was like the first, and that both together hold up the entire body of scripture and teaching that came before him. He would not let one stand without the other.

This is hard to hear if you have ever used devotion as a hiding place, saying the right prayers while avoiding the person who needs your attention. Jesus refused that split. Loving God shows up in how you treat the neighbor you did not choose, the one who annoys you, the one who has nothing to offer you back. If your faith never touches your neighbor, it has not yet reached the ground.

Practice. Before the day ends, do one specific, unasked-for kindness for someone you find hard to like.

May the love you carry for God find its feet in how you treat the person beside you this week. Go in peace, and take someone with you.

A teacher of the religious law once tried to trap Jesus with a simple question. Which commandment, out of all the hundreds recorded in the law, was the greatest? It was the kind of question meant to start an argument, since rabbis of the time debated endlessly over which rules outranked which. Jesus answered plainly. Love the Lord your God with all your heart, all your soul, and all your mind. Then, without pause, he added a second. Love your neighbor as yourself. He told the man that on these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets, meaning everything else written by Moses and the prophets rests on this pair like a door on its hinges.

Two Commands, One Weight

The detail worth sitting with is that Jesus was asked for one commandment and handed back two, insisting they carry equal weight. He called the second like the first, using the same word he used for God's command. That word choice seems deliberate. He is telling the lawyer, and anyone who has read this since, that love of God and love of neighbor rise and fall together. A person who prays every morning and steps over a struggling neighbor on the way to work has misunderstood what Jesus asked for.

Who Counts as Neighbor

The word neighbor in that sentence was doing more work than it first appears. In Jesus's world, plenty of teachers assumed neighbor meant someone from your own town, your own family, your own faith. Jesus kept widening the word until it included strangers, outsiders, and people the crowd around him would rather avoid. He never handed his listeners a shortlist of who qualified. He handed them a standard instead, treat the person in front of you the way you would want to be treated if the roles were reversed, broad enough to cover whoever happens to be standing there when you need it.

This fits how we try to live here. Nothing about a person's holiness comes from a title, a robe, a building, or even a book. Christ alone carries that weight. Loving your neighbor, then, has nothing to do with defending an institution's reputation or proving membership in the right group. It looks like listening to someone whose politics you cannot stand, or sitting with someone whose grief has no fix. None of that requires a title or a stage. It only requires attention.

If you have walked away from church, or never walked in, this teaching still holds you. Jesus did not ask the lawyer for a statement of faith before naming these two commands. He assumed the man already knew the words, since they came straight from Moses, and pushed him toward practice instead. You do not need certainty about every doctrine to love the person next to you today, and you do not need to give money you do not have or sign anything to prove it. You need to notice someone and do something for them that costs you a little.

The lawyer who asked the question probably wanted a ranking he could argue about. Jesus gave him something harder to debate and easier to fail, love that reaches both up and out at once. Try it this week in one specific place. Call the neighbor you have been avoiding, or sit for ten minutes with someone whose company does not come easily, and let this week be the one where love of God shows up as love of the person in front of you.
