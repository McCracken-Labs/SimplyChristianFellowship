The Beatitudes

This week we sit with the Beatitudes, where Jesus turns the world's ranking of people upside down and calls the overlooked ones blessed.

This Week’s Devotional

❦

“Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth.”Matthew 5:5, KJV

Meekness sounds like weakness until you watch it up close. The meek are not the loudest people in the room and rarely the ones pushing to be noticed. They hold real strength and set it down for the sake of someone else, over and over, without keeping score. Jesus blesses them because gentleness already carries the shape of the kingdom he came to bring.

If you have ever felt too quiet, too tired, or too ordinary to matter in a church, this verse speaks to you. The promise here has nothing to do with being impressive. Jesus names the meek as heirs of the earth, ahead of the loudest voice in the room or the person with the most followers. That promise holds even when no one else notices what you do today.

Practice. Today, let someone else go first, in traffic, in conversation, or in line, and pay attention to what it costs you.

May you find rest in gentleness today, and may you know that what the world overlooks in you, God calls blessed.

Jesus climbs a hillside in Galilee and sits down, and the people gather in close the way people do when someone is finally about to say something true. What comes next is a list, eight short blessings that read almost like a poem, and each one names a kind of person the world tends to walk past. The poor in spirit. Those who mourn. The meek. The hungry and thirsty for righteousness. The merciful. The pure in heart. The peacemakers. Even those persecuted for doing right. Nobody on that list is rich, powerful, admired, or in charge of anything. Jesus chooses to start his sermon with exactly these people, and nowhere else.

A Kingdom Turned Upside Down

In verse after verse, Jesus says, "Blessed are they that mourn: for they shall be comforted," and "Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy," and each line works the same way. He takes a condition nobody would choose for themselves, grief, or a hunger for a rightness the world rarely offers, and calls it blessed anyway. Jesus is naming who already stands closest to God, people whose lives the world rarely praises, and earthly power seems to have little to do with the ranking. The kingdom he describes runs on a different math than the one most of us learned growing up.

The verse this fellowship carries into the week, "Blessed are the meek: for they shall inherit the earth," sits right in the middle of that list, and it may be the hardest one to trust. Meekness often gets confused with being timid or having no opinion. The word behind it in the Greek text describes something closer to strength under control, like a powerful animal that lets itself be led. The meek are people who could assert themselves and choose restraint instead, again and again, because they trust something larger than their own need to win. This kind of strength rarely gets noticed at the time it is happening.

Only Christ Is Holy

This is where the Beatitudes line up closely with how this fellowship tries to see things. No book and no institution makes a person holy. Only Christ is holy, and he spent his teaching time pointing at the overlooked and calling them first in line. He did not bless the religious leaders of his day for their positions or their correct answers. He blessed people carrying grief and people hungry for justice, the ones trying to make peace in families and towns that had given up on it. Faith, in his description, looks like love in motion rather than a title someone holds or words someone recites.

If church has ever left you bruised, or if you carry doubts you have never said out loud, the Beatitudes were never meant to shame you back into line. Jesus spoke them for people standing outside the usual circles of approval, wondering if there was room for them anywhere. Jesus starts with the poor in spirit, the ones who know they do not have it all figured out, and tells them the kingdom already belongs to them. There is no entrance exam in this list, no correct doctrine required at the door.

So this week, watch for meekness in the ordinary corners of your life. Notice the parent who lets a small argument go rather than winning it, or the neighbor who forgives a slight without keeping score. When you catch someone practicing that kind of quiet strength, tell them plainly that you saw it.
