Salt and Light

This week we look at two everyday images Jesus used, salt and light, and what they ask of an ordinary life.

This Week’s Devotional

❦

“Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works, and glorify your Father which is in heaven.”Matthew 5:16, KJV

Jesus said this to ordinary people, not priests or scholars, farmers and fishermen sitting on a hillside with nothing special about them yet. You are the salt of the earth, he told them. You are the light of the world. He named something already true of them the moment they began to follow him, true before they had done anything to earn it, and it is still true of anyone who does the same today.

Salt only does its work once it touches the food. Light only helps once it is set where it can be seen. Faith works the same way, showing up in how you treat a tired cashier and how you show up for a neighbor with no one keeping score. Someone standing in that slow line, or sitting across from you in an argument, is often the only one who will ever know the difference it made.

Practice. Today, do one plain kind thing where someone else can actually see it happen, and let it speak for itself without explaining it.

May your life carry the flavor of kindness and the quiet glow of goodness done without fanfare, so that everyone you meet today meets a little more light because of you.

Picture an ordinary hillside crowd, farmers with dirt still under their nails, fishermen who smelled faintly of the lake, tax collectors nobody trusted, beggars nobody noticed. Jesus looked at this unremarkable gathering and said something startling. You are the salt of the earth. You are the light of the world. He stated it plainly, as something already true of them, a description of what had begun the moment they started following him, no ladder of piety required first. Nobody in that crowd had credentials worth mentioning, and that seems to be exactly why he chose them.

Salt Does Its Work Quietly

Salt in first century Galilee preserved fish and seasoned bread, and it worked by mixing into the food rather than sitting apart from it. A small amount changed the whole dish. Light worked in a similarly plain way, simply by being lit and set where people could see by it. Nobody lights a lamp and covers it with a basket, Jesus said, because a covered lamp helps no one find their way home in the dark. Salt left in the shaker is only salt by name. A lamp turned toward the wall gives no one anything to walk by. Both pictures describe something ordinary that only becomes useful once it is put to use where it can reach someone.

This is the sense in which the verse tells us to let our light shine before others, that they may see our good works and glorify our Father in heaven. Visible kindness tends to point past the person doing it, well beyond the moment someone happens to notice you. When a stranger helps someone change a tire in the rain, or forgives a debt nobody expected forgiven, the person watching rarely walks away thinking only about the individual who did it. Something larger tends to register, a sense of grace at work in an ordinary afternoon. The good work is real and specific, and yet it carries more than itself.

Faith You Can Taste and See

This fits how this fellowship tries to hold faith. No book, building, statue, or person carries holiness on its own, only Christ does, and Christ asks to be represented by how you treat the person standing in front of you rather than by a title or a doctrine recited word for word. Anyone can memorize a creed. Salt cannot fake its saltiness once it is tasted, and a lamp cannot fake its shining once the room goes dark. Living faith shows up in the small, plain moments where nobody is grading the performance, where the only witness might be one tired person having a hard day.

If organized religion has worn you down, or left you wary of people who talk about God while treating others poorly, this teaching offers something to hold onto that does not require trusting an institution or defending a position. It asks you to live in a way that leaves a mark on the people around you, plainly, where they can see it. Doubt does not disqualify anyone from this. You can question the resurrection and still carry soup to a sick neighbor, or wrestle with every doctrine you were ever taught and still let your light shine for someone having a hard week. Nobody checks your theology before letting you help carry their groceries up the stairs.

Good works done in the open let whatever good is already in you reach someone, instead of staying locked away where it helps no one. A kind word said only in private, or a check written quietly and never followed by a hand extended, still counts for something, but it changes very little about how a stranger sees the world. Look this week for one place where your ordinary kindness usually stays hidden, and bring it into the open instead.
