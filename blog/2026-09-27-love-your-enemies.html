Love Your Enemies

This week's reflection sits with the hardest line Jesus ever taught, love for the very people who have hurt you.

This Week’s Devotional

❦

“But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you.”Matthew 5:44, KJV

Loving people who love you back comes easily to almost everyone, whether they follow Christ or not. Jesus asks for something that goes further: love for people who have wronged you, a blessing spoken over those who curse you, and a prayer for those who use you badly. Anger toward an enemy feels justified, even righteous, in the moment. This teaching asks you to set that feeling down and pick up something else instead.

This is hard because forgiveness rarely feels natural while an enemy is still causing harm. But the teaching only asks for a genuine prayer, one honest attempt to wish someone well even while the wound is fresh. Small steps like this slowly loosen hatred's grip, on you as much as on anyone else.

Practice. Today, name one person who has hurt or angered you, and say a specific, honest prayer for their wellbeing before the day ends.

May the anger you carry find somewhere safe to rest tonight, and may you find, even now, a small mercy for the one who wronged you.

Almost every culture teaches people to love their own family and friends. This is not a hard sell. Parents protect their children instinctively, friends defend each other, communities close ranks around their own when trouble comes. Jesus took the conversation somewhere else entirely. In the middle of the Sermon on the Mount he told his listeners that loving people who already love you back earns no special credit. He asked instead for love toward enemies, a blessing spoken over people who curse you, prayer offered for people who treat you badly. Plenty of moral teachers before him had said that loving your own is good and right. Few had gone further to say the circle should include the person actively working against you. Of everything he taught, this line may be the most quoted and the least lived out.

Anger toward someone who has hurt you rarely responds to being told to calm down. It can feel like the only honest response left, a kind of loyalty to yourself and to whatever was taken from you. Jesus does not ask you to deny that the harm happened, or to pretend the person deserves your trust again. Loving an enemy in this sense means something narrower and more specific, turning your own heart away from wishing them harm, and toward wishing them well. That shift does not require forgetting, and it does not require staying anywhere close to someone who is actively dangerous. It asks only that the last word inside you, about that person, stop being a curse.

Retaliation has its own logic and its own momentum. A wrong done invites a wrong done back, and each side can point to the other as the one who started it, going back further than anyone can trace. Blessing someone who has cursed you, or praying honestly for someone who has mistreated you, breaks that chain in one specific spot, in one specific relationship, maybe the one keeping you up at night or coloring how you treat everyone else around you. A prayer said honestly for someone who wronged you leaves what they did exactly as it was, and quietly changes what you carry forward from it.

This fellowship holds that no book, building, or teacher is holy in itself. Only Christ is holy, and everything else, including how we read a verse like this one, gets weighed against his life and his way of loving people. Loving an enemy here is an invitation to follow the shape of Christ's own life, a life that absorbed cruelty without answering it with more cruelty. You do not need a title or a position in any church to practice this, and you do not need settled certainty about doctrine either. Doubt about all of it is welcome here, and so is starting small even when you fail at this more often than you keep it.

Faith in this teaching shows itself in what you do the next time someone genuinely wrongs you, more than in anything you might say about it beforehand. Nobody expects the anger to disappear overnight, and nobody here demands proof that you have mastered it. The teaching simply stays in front of you, patient and specific, waiting for the next chance to be tried again. Some weeks that chance comes from a stranger, and other weeks it comes from someone who has sat across your own dinner table for years. Pick one person today, someone whose name still stings a little when you think of it, and offer a single honest prayer for their good before the day ends.
